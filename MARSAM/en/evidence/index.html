Reading the evidence

Selected research on spirituality and mental health. Read findings alongside study design and limits of inference.

AI-assisted editorial draft. Scientific and native-language review are pending.

This selection is not a systematic review. Recency, methodological variety, Turkish contexts and contrasting findings informed inclusion. It is neither exhaustive nor an efficacy ranking.

What are we measuring?Association, change and treatment effectsSupport, struggles and possible harmCulture and life stageDisaster, grief and boundaries of careFrom evidence to professional practiceSelected evidence

What are we measuring?

Spirituality, religiosity, meaning and wellbeing are different constructs. When a spirituality measure incorporates wellbeing, its association with mental health may partly reflect conceptual overlap. Examine definitions and item content before interpreting a coefficient.

  • Meaning in Life Questionnaire: presence and search
  • DUREL: religious involvement and intrinsic religiosity
  • Brief RCOPE: two dimensions of religious coping

Association, change and treatment effects

Cross-sectional associations do not establish temporal order. Cohorts improve temporal information but cannot remove all confounding. In randomized trials, comparator choice and attrition matter. A nonsignificant overall difference in the religious CBT trial does not establish equivalence.

  • Religious attendance: three prospective cohorts
  • Religiously integrated versus conventional CBT
  • Meta-analytic evidence for spiritual adaptations

Support, struggles and possible harm

Belief and community may offer support or involve guilt, exclusion and conflict. Do not collapse positive and negative coping into one score. Spiritual struggle alone is not a disorder. Nonbelief is neither a deficit nor a mental illness.

  • Depression and anxiety in young people: distinct spiritual dimensions
  • Meaning and spiritual struggles in atheist samples
  • Religious and spiritual struggles

Culture and life stage

An association in one country, age group or religious tradition does not automatically transfer to another. Adolescence raises questions about family context, while later life brings loss and social support into focus. Who is absent from the sample matters alongside the average association.

  • Spirituality and mental health in German-speaking settings
  • Religion and adolescent mental health across cultures
  • Spirituality and mental health in later life

Disaster, grief and boundaries of care

Professional accounts after disasters help identify needs and service contexts. They do not show that an intervention reduces trauma symptoms. Spiritual support should follow the person’s wishes, avoid imposed meanings of loss and preserve access to needed mental health care.

  • Spiritual support needs after earthquakes
  • Spirituality in the Wake of Trauma

From evidence to professional practice

A research association alone does not justify introducing spirituality into a client’s care. Consider preferences, informed consent, professional competence and the intervention’s purpose together. Professional guidance offers an ethical framework. Claims of treatment benefit require controlled evidence.

  • Professional principles for spirituality and religion
  • Spiritual and religious competence
  • Client preferences for spiritual integration

Selected evidence

Meta-analysis · 2026

Spirituality and mental health in German-speaking settings

39 studies from German-speaking settings.

39 studies: a small association, r = 0.083.

Limits of inference: Correlation does not establish causation. Culture and measurement matter.

Source record
Systematic review · 2026

Religion and adolescent mental health across cultures

10 studies of adolescents across countries and religious contexts.

10 studies include beneficial, neutral and adverse associations.

Limits of inference: Contexts differ. Universal protection cannot be inferred.

Source record
Meta-analysis · 2023

Depression and anxiety in young people: distinct spiritual dimensions

Ages 10–24. 45 longitudinal studies and 29 intervention studies.

Spiritual wellbeing correlated inversely with depression. Religious importance showed no overall association.

Limits of inference: Study quality and intervention heterogeneity constrain conclusions.

Source record
Meta-analysis · 2022

Spirituality and mental health in later life

Observational research on adults aged 60 or older.

The review generally reported associations with better mental health.

Limits of inference: Observational findings do not establish treatment effects. Abstract and body report different study counts, so no total is reproduced here.

Source record
Prospective cohort · 2020

Religious attendance: three prospective cohorts

Three US cohorts following young, middle-aged and older adults.

Attendance was favourably associated with some subsequent mental health outcomes.

Limits of inference: US samples. Residual confounding remains possible. Attendance is not spirituality as a whole.

Source record
Cross-sectional study · 2018

Meaning and spiritual struggles in atheist samples

Atheist/theist comparisons in two samples of 3,978 undergraduates and 1,048 internet workers.

Meaning-related struggles were associated with distress among atheist participants too.

Limits of inference: Cross-sectional samples do not represent all nonreligious people.

Source record
Pilot randomized controlled trial · 2015

Religiously integrated versus conventional CBT

132 people with major depression and chronic medical illness. A 12-week comparison.

The 132-person pilot trial found no significant overall group difference.

Limits of inference: A nonsignificant difference does not prove equivalence.

Source record
Qualitative research · 2025

Spiritual support needs after earthquakes

Interviews with psychosocial support professionals after the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes.

Professional interviews explore spiritual support needs after earthquakes.

Limits of inference: Interviews do not establish intervention efficacy.
